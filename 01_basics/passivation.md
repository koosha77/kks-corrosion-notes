# Passivation and depassivation of steel in concrete

## 1. Passive layer

In fresh, alkaline concrete (pH ~ 12.5–13.5), a thin oxide film (`γ-Fe₂O₃` / `Fe₃O₄`) forms on the rebar surface. This **passive layer** reduces the corrosion rate to a negligible level (< 1 µm/year). As long as the pore solution stays alkaline and chloride-free, the reinforcement is electrochemically protected.

*Deutsch:* Passivschicht / Passivierung.

## 2. Two main depassivation mechanisms

### 2.1 Carbonation (Karbonatisierung)

CO₂ from the atmosphere diffuses into the concrete and reacts with calcium hydroxide:

```
Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂  →  CaCO₃ + H₂O
```

The pH drops from ~ 12.5 to ~ 9 in the carbonated zone. Once the carbonation front reaches the rebar, the passive layer breaks down and *uniform* corrosion can start.

Key parameter: **carbonation depth** `x_c(t) ≈ K · √t`, where `K` depends on concrete quality, exposure class (XC1…XC4), and curing.

### 2.2 Chloride ingress (Chlorideintrag)

Chlorides from de-icing salts or seawater diffuse through the pore system. When the chloride content at the steel surface exceeds a critical threshold (commonly **0.4 % by mass of cement**), the passive film breaks down *locally* and **pitting corrosion** initiates.

This is the dominant mechanism for:

- Bridge decks and parking garages (de-icing salt)
- Marine and coastal structures (XS exposure classes)

## 3. Why KKS becomes relevant

Once depassivation occurs, repair alone may not stop the macro-cell corrosion driven by chloride contamination. **Cathodic protection** shifts the steel potential into the immune region, suppressing further metal dissolution even if chlorides remain in the concrete.
